Once, in my years as a critic and researcher of the Nueva Trova, I heard a songwriter remark that one of his songs had aged. One of its lines named, quite specifically, the black-market exchange rate for foreign currency. A few years after he wrote it, that rate had shot up astronomically, far beyond what the line said.
I don't believe the song has aged, because every account of a period is memory, a milestone on record; but something similar happens with this novel. The literary reality it describes, set more or less in the second decade of the twenty-first century, was far surpassed, for the worse, by lived reality, especially the most recent. Quite a few of the situations the book describes, sometimes admiringly and sometimes critically, were overturned by what happened afterwards in real life.
For various reasons this book has remained unpublished. Although some of the country's circumstances have practically disappeared and are no longer as the novel tells them, the author does not fear that these pages have become useless. Had life improved, the book would be a reminder, a warning, an alert against certain ills that must not return. No story that can tell us something about the past, however recent, is worthless. If life has grown worse, that same memory serves as a lighthouse, a light that reminds us, when the time comes to reinvent everything, of what we were, what we want to be, and what we do not, in the future.
Beyond the portraits it draws, outdated or not, better or worse, every work of art hopes to stir something in whoever receives it. In this case, realities aside, if the text manages to raise a single question, past, present or yet to come, this scribe will be satisfied. There will be a novel that did its job.
